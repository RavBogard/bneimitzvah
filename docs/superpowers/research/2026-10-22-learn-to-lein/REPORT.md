# How people learn to lein: research and recommendations

2026-10-23. Part A of the Learn to lein handoff. Written by Claude (Cowork) for Daniel and for Claude Code. Sources are in SOURCES.md; every claim in the body below is marked [primary/verified], [secondary] or [inference]. The design that follows from this is in COURSE.md and ACTIVITIES.md.

## Conclusions, one page

1. Teach whole phrases, and teach the two endings first. Every curriculum found, Reform and Conservative, seminary and congregational, opens with sof pasuk and etnachta, then zakef katan, because those three phrases are in nearly every verse and the endings fix the key. Conjunctives are taught inside the phrase they lead into, which is exactly how the site's engine already groups them. The chart's order is a display order, not a learning order. [A1, primary]

2. Listen first, then the names, then the words. Practitioners agree on the sequence: hear the motif sung, sing the trope names to it, find it in a real verse, then sing the words. The trope song is a review mnemonic that teachers use after the families are known, not lesson one. [A1, primary; no controlled studies exist]

3. Make every screen a small question. Retrieval practice beats re-listening (g = 0.50 to 0.73 with feedback); recognition first, then recall, then production. The course's listening screens come before the first drill and nowhere else. [A2, primary]

4. Space the review. A modified Leitner schedule (same session, then 1, 3, 7, 16, 35 days; a miss drops two boxes) is enough for eleven families and needs about eighty lines of code; FSRS or SM-2 are an upgrade path, not a v1 need. For a learner with a date, keep gaps to 10 to 20 percent of the time left. [A2, primary for the spacing results; the schedule is inference]

5. Block briefly, then interleave. A new family gets a short block of clear examples, then it is mixed at once with the families it is most often confused with. Interleaving wins for perceptual categories, including musical ones; the exception is beginners with weak pitch perception, who need the short block first. [A2, primary]

6. Feedback is about the melody, immediately, and shows the right answer. Right/wrong alone teaches nothing; replaying the correct phrase and the chosen one, then re-asking, does. No score on screen. [A2, primary]

7. Colour is a helper, not the teacher. Colour-coded notation showed little lasting benefit; a contour line in sync with the sound helped tone learners more, and two visual cues together added nothing. So each family carries its name, its chart colour and a small contour mark, and the contour is what animates. [A2, primary; the application to trope is inference]

8. Short sessions, most days. Three to five minutes on a phone, one new family per lesson, at most about four families active in the first weeks. [A2, secondary and inference]

9. Warmth without the banned mechanics is available and is what the evidence favours. Duolingo's own data say spaced review and difficulty-matching raised retention and learning; its streaks are the mechanic that moves daily use most and the one the rule bans. The most rigorous studies of badges and leaderboards found no lasting motivational gain and some harm. The course keeps a single path, plain unit names, re-asking missed items, a mistakes set, spaced review, a visible "can hear 6 of 10", and one quiet moment; it drops streaks, points, hearts, leagues, mascots, fanfare and pep talk. The two mechanics worth asking Daniel about are a streak and a daily goal (QUESTIONS-FOR-DANIEL.md, 1 and 2). [A3, mixed]

10. Torah first, then haftarah as "the same phrases, a new tune". Every curriculum teaches Torah first; the Reform handbooks found make Torah the main task; nothing supports haftarah-first for students. One cantor advises adults to start with haftarah because the marks are printed, which the site makes moot. The haftarah path is unlocked after the Torah endings for anyone whose own reading is a haftarah. [A1, primary; the soft lock is inference]

11. The commonest beginner errors are about position and pausing, not pitch: pashta and kadma, mahpach and yetiv, azla and geresh told apart by where the mark sits; pausing on a conjunctive; running through a disjunctive; wrong stress. The v1 drill "where does the phrase end" and the later "same sign, different place" target these directly. [A1, primary]

12. Singing back can be built cheaply in the browser (pitchy, 0BSD, a few hundred lines), but only as a mirror: two melody lines, no score, nothing stored or sent. A numeric grade would be dishonest for free-rhythm chant sung in a different key by a child against an adult reader. A nonprofit congregation is very likely outside COPPA, and on-device-only processing is not "collection" under FTC guidance, but recording a child's voice still needs Daniel's yes, so it is a question, not a feature. [A4, primary for the law and the libraries; the honesty argument is inference]

13. What no existing app does, and this course can: build from one motif to a phrase to a verse, drill recognition, give immediate feedback, schedule review, show progress, and tie every lesson to this week's reading. The Torah apps that exist are reference players; their users' complaint is that "phrases don't build in complexity". [A3, primary]

14. Gaps in the evidence. There is no controlled study of cantillation pedagogy; Binder's, Rosowsky's and Ne'eman's own teaching orders could not be verified from the books themselves; the trope-song lyric in the brief could not be matched to a published sheet; and the children's-voice pitch-range figures for sing-back are from general knowledge, not a checked source. None of these changes the design. [inference]

## A1. How cantors and tutors teach trope

Most book contents here were seen only through tables of contents, reviews or catalogue pages, and the tags say which. The project's grammar files treat the melodies as Binder/Avery "Reform Standard" cells (44 haftarah, 51 Torah), use Jacobson-style phrase sets (66 haftarah phrases on Cantor Sarah Bolts's site) as the patterns a teacher would list, and propose recording neutral units, possibly sung on trope names, that double as the learner drill [primary/verified, project files]; they hold little pedagogy beyond that.

### The zarka table and the trope song

Malin (Yuval 10, 2016) writes that the melodies "are typically learned first with the te'amim and their names", then drilled phrase by phrase, and cites Binder, Cohen, Jacobson and Portnoy/Wolff as teaching in combined phrases [primary/verified]. His footnote 13 describes an alternative: all the te'amim at once in the order of the zarka table [primary/verified, paraphrase of the footnote], a "traditional order of recitation" that "bears some relation to the groups in which the signs are likely to occur" but varies by community [primary/verified, Wikipedia].

The Ashkenazi table with its conjunctives (Open Siddur, from the 1904 Mikraot Gedolot) runs [primary/verified]: *kadma munach zarka munach segol · munach | munach revi'i · mahpach pashta zakef katon · zakef gadol · mercha tipcha munach etnachta · pazer · telisha ketana · telisha gedola · kadma v'azla · azla geresh · gershayim · darga tevir · yetiv · psik · mercha tipcha mercha sof pasuk*, then the rare *shalshelet · karnei farah · mercha kefula · yerach ben yomo*. American trope songs keep this skeleton and differ mainly in which munachs they keep [inference; no published lyric sheet confirmed it].

The trope song is a mnemonic, not a phrase grammar: zarka and segol lead by tradition, not because they are common, and every modern curriculum found teaches them late [inference from the sequences below]. Trope-song tracks are offered as review after the families, not as lesson one; Natick calls them "optional extras" [primary/verified].

### The textbooks

Jacobson, *Chanting the Hebrew Bible* (2nd ed., JPS 2017), is ordered by syntax from the top of the hierarchy down (siluk and etnachta, tippecha, conjunctives, zakef, segol, tevir, revia, pashta, zarka, geresh, legarmeh, pazer, telishah gedolah), then "The Pedagogy of Cantillation" (pp. 447–54); melodies appear only in chapter 6 [primary/verified, table of contents]. A reviewer calls melody an addition "to aid memory and promote proper phrasing" [primary/verified, Leman, JBTS 2018]. Hebrew College's High Holy Day course (Treitman, 2019) teaches from the 2005 Student Edition in the order siluk and etnahta, tevir, zakef, segol, revia (students "color-code their reading by the patterns learned so far"), legarmeh and geresh, telishah gedolah and pazer [primary/verified, syllabus]. Jacobson says many b'nei mitzvah teachers "teach the wrong things", and "It's not the notes that matter most … It's the rhythm" [primary/verified, Jewish Standard 2015]; printed pitches "need not be imitated slavishly" [secondary, via Malin].

In Portnoy and Wolff (Transcontinental, Reform), vol. 1 Lessons 1–4 teach "the three main types of trope clauses: etnachta, sof-pasuk, and katon", the rest is consulted as needed, Appendix H gives 41 clause varieties, and the CD alternates trope alone, words, and repetition [secondary, reader reviews]. Vol. 2 (haftarot and megillot) is "a step-by-step guide" with 2 CDs [primary/verified, publisher listing]. Their notation follows Binder and Cantor Lawrence Avery [secondary, Malin], making it the direct source of the project's cells [inference]. Vol. 2 p. 26 gives the munach sequences *mahpach-pashta-munach-zakef katan*, *mercha-tipcha-munach-etnachta*, *munach-zarka-munach-segol*, *munach-munach-revia* and *munach-munach-(pazer)-telisha* [secondary, via Wikipedia "Munach"].

Binder's teaching order could not be verified (only catalogue data was found) [primary/verified, catalogue]; Rosowsky (1957) is a reference "for specialists" [primary/verified], not a lesson sequence [inference]; nothing was found on Ne'eman's order.

Every sequence opens with sof pasuk and etnachta, in either order, and teaches zakef katon third [inference from the table]. The LBC's reason: these phrases are "the building blocks of nearly every verse", and rare signs wait until common patterns are secure [primary/verified]. Tipcha alone is 24% of disjunctives in Jacobson's frequency chart [secondary, via project file/Malin]. Teachers teach the clause, with conjunctives inside the phrase they lead into, matching the project's convention that tipcha, pashta, yetiv and zarka belong to the phrase after them [inference from the table]. Revia, tevir and kadma v'azla sit in positions 4–6; segol/zarka, telisha, pazer, gershayim and zakef gadol come late; the end-of-reading cadence and rare signs last [inference from the table]. Geresh/gershayim and telisha gedola often get their own groups, which the project's 11 families do not name [inference].

### Torah and haftarah

Torah comes first in the curricula found. Natick finishes Torah trope by early December, practises on maftir portions, then teaches haftarah blessings and trope [primary/verified]; Har Shalom and Temple Sholom (Greenwich) list Torah first, and Beth Or, Maple Glen PA (URJ logo) runs parallel nine-part series [primary/verified]. Reform b'nei mitzvah usually chant both, with Torah as the main task. Temple Sinai Denver (Reform) asks for 12–20 Torah and 6–10 haftarah verses, says to "focus mainly" on Torah, and wants haftarah mastered by the second rehearsal [primary/verified, handbook]; Temple Micah (city and movement not stated) asks for about 20 Torah verses plus part of the haftarah [primary/verified]; ReformJudaism.org says Reform congregations commonly read "a selection of verses" [primary/verified]. Nothing found supports the idea that Reform students learn haftarah first [inference from absence]. One cantor (Kliger, 2013) advises adults to start with haftarah because "the markings are written into the text"; another (Wissot) says start with Torah because people already know some of it [primary/verified]. A Conservative shul in Millburn NJ (2015) dropped the required haftarah for Torah trope, a weekly skill [primary/verified].

Both modes share one phrase grammar: Torah is major and rests on 2; haftarah is minor, with sof pasuk its only cadence on 1 [primary/verified, project files citing Malin]. Family lessons carry over and only the melody table changes [inference]. No account of students mixing up the tunes was found, though the risk is plausible when both are learned in one year [inference].

### Common beginner errors

- **Pashta vs kadma (same glyph).** Pashta sits on the last letter, doubled on the stressed syllable when stress is not final; kadma sits on the stressed syllable [primary/verified, Wikipedia; LBC; BEKI; Mandel]. Natick draws a placement box on each flashcard [primary/verified].
- **Mahpach vs yetiv (same "<").** Yetiv is prepositive, with no pashta after it [primary/verified, Wikipedia; Natick; LBC; Mandel]. Munach vs mahpach is a shape problem no source discusses [inference].
- **Azla vs geresh (same glyph).** Stress and context decide [primary/verified, LBC].
- **Stress (mil'el/mil'ra).** English speakers stress the last syllable; double pashta marks a mil'el word [primary/verified, LBC]. Upbeat notes follow the stress, so a wrong stress breaks the whole cell [primary/verified, LBC], though it rarely changes meaning [primary/verified, Mann 2023].
- **Pausing.** Readers still pause on a conjunctive or skip the pause at a disjunctive (Mandel, Num 28:14) [primary/verified]. Bentley has students slash after each separator: "pause at each mafsik and keep chanting through each m'chaber" [primary/verified].
- **Tipcha** starts higher than mercha and is harder to pitch [primary/verified, LBC].
- **Conjunctives change their tune** with the next disjunctive [primary/verified, LBC; project file lists 9+ munach contexts]; over-singing munach "may result in too much emphasis" (Jacobson, relayed 1995) [secondary].
- **Sof pasuk.** Teachers disagree whether Torah sof pasuk ends 1→5 below (Binder/Portnoy-Wolff) or 3→1 (Kalib and others) [primary/verified, project file], so a recording and a cantor may differ [inference].
- **Zakef katon vs revia.** A pair of dots vs a single diamond [primary/verified, Wikipedia]; that they are confused is [inference].

Teachers catch errors with placement flashcards, family highlighting, singing names before words, and comparing against a recording; no formal error-checking protocol was found.

### What works: practitioner evidence

Listening comes first. Kol Kore (Masorti) goes from hearing and repeating each sign to finding it in the text, combinations and whole verses, and lets beginners learn a short maftir "as a song" [primary/verified]. TropeTrainer: "Learn by ear before you try to read from bare letters", and "slow the audio right down at first" [primary/verified]. Temple Sholom calls recordings "the primary method" [primary/verified]. The LBC, Cantor Lupul and Natick all have learners sing the trope names before the words [primary/verified]; Natick also uses flashcards ("see the symbol, know the name, then sing the tune") and the V'ahavta sung on trope names [primary/verified].

Colour by family is near-universal: Natick uses six highlighter colours, Bentley four, Treitman has students colour-code by pattern, Kol Kore colours each trope, Bolts uses the "Keshet Te'amim" scheme, and TropeTrainer highlights trope groups [primary/verified, the last via the project file]. In "cantillation aerobics" (Cantor Ruth Katz Green, after Cantor Sharon Brown-Levy) students reach up for marks above the word and dip as the melody falls, and graduates of the 12-week course read from the scroll [primary/verified, 2010]. A 5th-grade class taught with gestures, piano and diagrams "retained the trope names and sounds better than … the Hebrew alphabet" [primary/verified, New Voices 2009], and "Tricks of the Trope" (Hazzan Arianne Brown) pairs melodies with sign-shaped gestures [primary/verified, jewfaq].

Practice runs 10–15 minutes a day (Natick, Temple Micah), or "a little every day, even one verse" (TropeTrainer) [primary/verified]. An 11th-century Genizah "trope trainer" set notes on verses from Gen 1:1 onward chosen to cover every note, including rare ones [primary/verified, Cambridge]; the LBC reuses Gen 1 and Exod 25:23–40 for the same reason [primary/verified]. Kol Kore ends with practice from text without trope or vowels [primary/verified]. No controlled studies were found; all of this is practitioner report [inference from search].

## A2. Learning science that fits

The learners, 12-year-olds and adults, need to recognise about 11 melodic families by ear, link each to its marks, and chant a verse. No study tests these methods on cantillation, so every application to trope is an inference.

### Retrieval practice

Dunlosky et al. (2013) rated only practice testing and distributed practice "high utility" of ten techniques; rereading, highlighting and summarising rated low [secondary: APS press release]. Roediger and Karpicke (2006) found restudying won at five minutes (81% vs 75%) but testing won at one week (56% vs 42%; one study plus three tests 61%, four study sessions 40%). Restudy's better immediate scores are why learners misjudge it [primary/verified]. Rowland's (2014) meta-analysis of 61 studies and 159 effect sizes found testing beat restudy at g = 0.50: 0.73 with feedback vs 0.39 without, 0.69 at intervals of a day or more vs 0.41 for shorter, and 0.61 for cued recall vs 0.29 for recognition practice [primary/verified]. Adesope et al. (2017) found practice tests beat restudying "and all other comparison conditions" [primary/verified, abstract only]. But a difficulty only helps when the learner can mostly succeed; otherwise it becomes "undesirable" (Bjork and Bjork 2011) [primary/verified]. For a beginner that suggests recognition ("which of these 3 marks did you hear?"), then cued recall ("sing the motif for this mark", "pick the melody from 4 audio choices"), then free production, ending with mark → sound items because chanting from text runs that way [inference]. An audio trial is a retrieval trial only if the learner commits to an answer first; "listen again" screens are restudy [inference].

### Spaced repetition

Across 839 assessments from 317 experiments, spaced study averaged 47.3% recall vs 36.7% massed, only 12 of 271 comparisons showed no benefit, and the best gap grew with the retention interval to a month or more for 6-month retention (Cepeda et al. 2006) [primary/verified]. With over 1,350 learners tested up to a year later, the best gap was about 20–40% of the retention interval for a 1-week test and 5–10% for a 1-year test, and too-short gaps cost more than too-long ones (Cepeda et al. 2008) [primary/verified]. Applied here: review after 1.5–3 days to remember a family a week later, and spread later reviews 2–5 weeks apart for a bar/bat mitzvah 6–12 months out [inference from Cepeda 2008]. One stack of 20 flashcards gave 65% next-day recall vs 38% for four stacks of 5; 90% did better with the large stack but 72% preferred small ones (Kornell 2009) [secondary: education blog report].

Expanding schedules helped recall at 10 minutes and equal intervals at 2 days, and delaying the first retrieval mattered most (Karpicke and Roediger 2007) [primary/verified]; expanding schedules led only numerically in Cepeda et al. 2006 (62.0% vs 58.6%, p = .61) [primary/verified]. Any spacing, and not testing instantly after teaching, matters more than the exact schedule [inference].

For motor skill, Brady (2004) found mixed practice beat blocked by an average 0.38 (0.57 in labs, 0.19 in the field), but only 0.09 for children and 0.10 for high-schoolers against 0.50 for adults [primary/verified]. UT Austin music research studies sleep-dependent consolidation of keyboard melodies [secondary: titles and topic summaries only]. Pitch-matching error rates run 48.7% in kindergarten, 20.5% in grade 6 and 41.8% in adults, and about 45% of sixth-graders but only about 5% of adults matched at least 90% of pitches, which the authors attribute to practice (Demorest and Pfordresher 2015) [primary/verified]. Vocal skill appears to need upkeep, which supports sung review, not only listening [inference].

Of browser-sized algorithms, Leitner boxes move a card up one box when right and back to box 1 when wrong, and SM-2 multiplies intervals of 1 day, then 6, by a per-card easiness factor starting at 2.5 [secondary: Wikipedia]. FSRS uses 19 parameters normally fitted to large review logs [secondary: Borretti write-up]. Duolingo's half-life regression cut prediction error over 45% against Leitner-style baselines and raised daily engagement 12%, but needs millions of training examples [primary/verified]. For about 11 families and 40–80 items the research proposes modified Leitner: a new or failed item returns in the same session after 2–4 others, then at 1, 3, 7, 16 and 35 days; a wrong answer drops two boxes (minimum box 1), since a full reset is harsh for near-threshold perceptual items; sound → mark, mark → sound and sung items are separate cards; and gaps are capped at 10–20% of the days left before a known performance date [inference, from Cepeda 2008]. FSRS with default weights is the upgrade if items reach the hundreds [inference].

### Interleaving vs blocking

Interleaved study of 12 artists' paintings gave 61% correct identification of new paintings vs 35% blocked (59% vs 36% between groups), yet 78% judged massing as good or better (Kornell and Bjork 2008) [primary/verified]; an online replication with 123 people found d = 0.37 (pooled 0.39), and 71% again preferred massing (Verkoeijen and Bouwmeester 2014) [primary/verified]. Birnbaum et al. (2013) found interleaving won .36 vs .21 when categories sat back to back, but unrelated trivia between them erased the gain (.29 vs .28): contrast between neighbours is the active ingredient [primary/verified]. Brunmair and Richter's (2019) meta-analysis found g = 0.42 overall and 0.67 for paintings, but blocking was better for word learning (g = −0.39) [primary/verified]. Interleaving helps most when categories are similar and examples within them vary [primary/verified].

In music, novices classifying composers' styles learned better interleaved, while most believed the reverse (Wong, Low, Kang and Lim 2020) [primary/verified]. For melodic intervals, schedules came out equal when learners used reference songs and sang along, and interleaving clearly won without those aids (Wong, Chen and Lim 2021) [primary/verified]. This is the closest published analogue to learning trope families by ear [inference].

Blocking can help beginners. Weak pitch perceivers learning pitch-contour words did worse with trial-by-trial variety (d = 1.30 against high-variability training) and improved when talker variety was blocked (d = 1.65), while strong perceivers benefited from variety (d = 0.73) (Perrachione et al. 2011) [primary/verified]. Novices also do better with low variability in maths (Likourezos, Kalyuga and Sweller 2019) [primary/verified], and easy high-contrast trials make hard discriminations learnable (Ahissar and Hochstein 2004, visual work) [primary/verified]. That points to a single-voice block of 3–5 clear hearings per new family, then immediate interleaving with its most confusable neighbours [inference].

### Chunking and cognitive load

Working memory holds about 3–5 meaningful chunks (Cowan 2001) [secondary], and the right amount of variety for novices shifts with expertise [primary/verified via Likourezos et al. 2019; the broader theory is secondary]. Dowling (1978) found listeners remember new melodies by contour before exact intervals, and confuse same-contour tunes in the same mode at chance [primary/verified]. Families with similar contours should be the most confused pairs [inference]. A disjunctive with its usual conjunctives is a natural chunk; one new family per lesson, two at most, with about 4 in play in early weeks [inference from Cowan; no trope-specific data].

### Feedback

Across 435 studies feedback averages d = 0.48: corrective 0.46, rewards or punishment alone 0.24, high-information feedback (what was wrong and how to fix it) 0.99 (Wisniewski, Zierer and Hattie 2020) [primary/verified]. Over a third of feedback interventions reduced performance, self-focused feedback being weaker or harmful (Kluger and DeNisi 1996) [primary/verified]. Right/wrong-only feedback gave no gain, while showing the correct answer after an error raised one-week retention 494% over no feedback, and feedback after correct answers made little difference (Pashler et al. 2005) [primary/verified]. Slightly delayed feedback beat immediate (Butler, Karpicke and Roediger 2007) [primary/verified]. After an error, the natural response is the correct motif, then chosen and correct back to back, one plain line naming the difference, and a re-test a few items later [inference].

### Dual coding, colour and contour cues

Coding an item both verbally and nonverbally improves memory (Clark and Paivio 1991) [primary/verified]; redundant material adds load (Mayer) [secondary]. Colour-coded notation shows weak results: 92 beginning wind players over 12 weeks showed "no clear advantage", though about 65% found colour easier (Rogers 1991) [primary/verified], and 134 first and second graders over 23 weeks showed a small advantage, mainly while reading coloured notation, with 78% liking it (*JRME* 1996, author not shown, probably Rogers) [primary/verified, abstract]. The benefit may not survive removing the colour [inference]. For Mandarin tones, 303 novices in 3-week web training improved with every visual cue, contour lines slightly beat colour, and colour plus contour added nothing (Godfroid, Lin and Ryu 2017) [primary/verified]; rise-and-fall arrows helped most with audio and for the least experienced (Wei et al. 2022) [primary/verified]. No study on colour-coding cantillation was found.

### Ear training and singing

Eight sessions of many-voice Mandarin tone training improved 8 American learners by 21%, with transfer to new words (18%) and new voices plus words (25%) still present at six months (Wang et al. 1999) [primary/verified]; perception-only training also improved production by 18%, contour more than pitch height (Wang et al. 2003) [primary/verified]. About 10–16% of adults are poor-pitch singers with normal perception; the deficit is in mapping pitch to voice (Pfordresher and Brown 2007) [primary/verified]. In 20 minutes of training, only real-time visual pitch feedback plus doubled audio produced a significant gain, clearest on 4-note melodies (Berglin, Pfordresher and Demorest 2022) [primary/verified, abstract; no effect sizes given]. Deliberate practice explains about 21% of variance in music performance (Macnamara et al. 2014) [primary/verified]. Brief motif-length singing with a visible pitch trace is the evidence-backed route to production [inference].

### Session length and motivation

No strong evidence fixes a micro-lesson length [secondary]. Duolingo reports bingers were "much more likely to quit" [primary, company blog 2017] and targets at least 15 minutes a day [primary, company blog]. In a 6-month natural experiment with 287 learners, frequency of use predicted gains better than total minutes [primary/verified, abstract]. That favours 3–7 minute sessions of 15–25 trials on most days [inference].

Competence, autonomy and relatedness support intrinsic motivation (Ryan and Deci 2000) [secondary]. Expected tangible rewards lowered later free-choice engagement (d = −0.34), and positive verbal feedback raised it for college students (0.43) but not reliably for children (0.11) (Deci, Koestner and Ryan 1999) [primary/verified]. Gamification showed g = 0.49 cognitive, 0.36 motivational and 0.25 behavioural, but the last two lost significance in the most rigorous studies (Sailer and Homner 2020) [primary/verified]. Mandatory badges and a leaderboard lowered intrinsic motivation, satisfaction and exam scores (Hanus and Fox 2015) [secondary]. Duolingo reports a streak "wager" raised day-7 retention 14% and a weekend protector cut streak loss 5% [primary, company-reported, no sample sizes]; a broken streak lowers engagement, less so if repairable (Silverman and Barasch 2023) [secondary: university news summaries]. People speed up near a goal, even an illusory one (Kivetz et al. 2006) [primary/verified, abstract]; slow-looking early progress bars raised drop-out across 32 experiments (Villar et al. 2013) [primary/verified]. No evidence on celebration animations was found; Kluger and DeNisi suggest self-focused praise can lower performance [primary/verified for Kluger and DeNisi; inference for its application here].

### Duolingo

The 2022 redesign replaced the skill tree with one path. Each circle is a level of "several lessons", units carry plain-language headers ("get directions", not "City 3"), tips live in a per-unit "guidebook", and review and practice are mixed into the path [primary/verified, Duolingo blog]. Critics objected to lost choice [secondary, UX Collective 2022]. Lessons are "bite-sized lessons that take just a few minutes", moving from easier to harder uses of the same content [primary/verified, Duolingo teaching method 2023], roughly 10 to 15 exercises in practice [inference]. Types include "What do you hear?" and "Type what you hear", with a turtle button for slow replay [secondary, community wiki]. Listening is staged: audio first comes with written words, later with none, and other scripts get sound-to-character items [primary/verified, Duolingo blog 2026].

Hearts took one of five per wrong answer to "keep you from moving too quickly" [primary/verified, Duolingo blog 2024]; since July 2025 an energy system charges every exercise, more for wrong answers, which users read as a paywall [secondary, Android Authority 2025]. A missed exercise returns later in the lesson, which cannot finish until it is answered correctly [inference: well known, no official page found]. A practice set "review[s] previous mistakes" [primary/verified, Practice Hub guide 2026]. A banner turns green, or red with the correct answer [inference].

Half-life regression schedules practice "on the verge of forgetting" and in A/B tests raised daily retention 9.5% for practice sessions, 1.7% for lessons and 12% overall [primary/verified, Duolingo blog 2016], with prediction error cut "45%+" [primary/verified, Settles and Meeder 2016]. Birdbrain predicts whether a learner will get an item right and serves "just the right difficulty level", raising both learning and return visits [primary/verified, Duolingo blog 2020]. In version 2 a wrong answer lowers the learner's ability estimate and raises the item's difficulty, Elo-style; about 20% of started lessons had been abandoned, often at hard material, and engagement and learning rose together, which the authors call unusual [primary/verified, IEEE Spectrum 2023].

Much of the motivational layer is what the course's tone rules exclude. Lessons earn 25 XP plus combos and boosts [secondary, Duoplanet]; the end screen has a mascot animation and fanfare [inference]; streak milestones turn the mascot into a flaming phoenix with a share card [primary/verified, Duolingo blog 2022]. Learners on a 7-day streak are 2.4 times more likely to return next day, and separating streak from daily goal raised day-14 retention 3.3% and 7+ day streaks over 40%, though "fewer learners were actually reaching their daily goals" [primary/verified, Duolingo blog 2020]. Relabelling "continue" as "commit to my goal" "significantly increased retention" [secondary, Lenny's Newsletter 2024]. Leagues of about 30 learners promote and demote weekly [primary/verified, Duolingo blog 2023]; one analysis credits loss aversion with +25% lesson completion, method unstated [secondary, Deconstructor of Fun].

What fits: short lessons, one path with an obvious next step, plain unit names, text-then-no-text listening, feedback that shows the answer, re-asking missed items, a mistakes set, spaced review, and difficulty near the learner's edge, which simple rules can approximate [inference]. What clashes: streaks, leagues, XP, hearts or energy that punish mistakes, mascot and fanfare, guilt copy, "Insane" goal tiers, ads and share cards [inference].

### Hebrew-reading, Torah and bar/bat mitzvah apps

PocketTorah (Russel Neiss and Rabbi Charlie Schwartz, 2012) is free, ad-free, open source and human-voiced, chanting every Torah and haftarah portion with word-by-word highlighting in vowelled or scroll text [secondary, JTA 2012; primary/verified, App Store]. ### Language-tone trainers

Beyond the tone-training studies in A2, a moving pitch arrow played with the sound helped newcomers most, learners less and native speakers not at all, most with varied speech [primary/verified, Wei et al. 2022]. ToneDetector shows a syllable's pitch shape beside a sample and leaves the judgement to the learner's eye [primary/verified, App Store]. In Praat, real contours are messier than textbook diagrams, so exactly matching a native line is a poor target; the display is better for seeing what your own tones do [secondary, Sinosplice 2008]. Pleco's developer found in informal tests that tone colours don't help recall "as much as we would hope" and many users ignore them; he was trying a continuous line across a word [primary/verified, Pleco forums; secondary, AllSet Learning: "the jury is still out"]. Pinyin Trainer runs listening-only minimal pairs and Tone Perfect tracks the hardest tone pairs [secondary]. HelloChinese's voice scoring is "relatively basic"; Speechling pairs record-and-compare with a human coach replying within 24 hours [secondary]. ELSA colours each sound green, yellow or red with a percentage, flags words at 80 or below, adds badges and applause, and one reviewer passed stress drills by saying "blah" [secondary, All Language Resources 2024].

Across all three groups, what brings people back without pressure is short finishable sessions (five items for ELSA, 10 minutes a day for Functional Ear Trainer), an immediate right answer with its reason, items near the edge of ability, review of recent mistakes, a visible path, and a plain record of progress [primary/verified, secondary and inference as above]. ## A4. Singing back: checking your own chant in the browser

Can a learner record a 2–6 word, 2–6 second phrase and compare it honestly, in the browser and with no new paid services, against a human reference recording? Technically yes, and cheaply. Honesty is the hard part: trope is free-rhythm chant, the reference reader is an adult man, and the learner is often a child. Two melody lines side by side with no grade is honest and buildable; a grade is not [inference]. Children's singing pitch ranges, published "accurate singing" thresholds and iOS permission defaults were not checked, and claims on them are marked [inference].

### Pitch detection in the browser

`getUserMedia` opens the microphone only on HTTPS, asks permission at least the first time, and shows an indicator while in use [primary/verified, MDN]. The Web Audio API then gives the page raw audio, and its `AudioContext` must start inside a tap or click or it stays suspended [primary/verified, MDN]. For a short phrase, recording into memory and analysing the whole clip after it stops removes almost all CPU and latency worry on phones; a live follow-the-line display is possible but unnecessary at first [inference].

SwiftF0's author, to be read with care, benchmarks desktop CPU cost per second of audio at 26.5 ms for SwiftF0, 284 ms for pYIN and 1,102 ms for CREPE, with average clean raw pitch accuracy of 90%, 87.5% and 70.9%, and SwiftF0 holding up best at 10 dB noise [primary/verified]. A 2019 study of trained singers found YIN the most precise but most hurt by echo, gross errors rising from 0.9% to about 7% with mild reverberation, and sopranos tracked 2–4× better than baritones; it included no children or untrained voices [primary/verified]. No browser benchmark on a mid-range Android phone was found; the estimate is well under a second for pitchy on a 6-second clip on a 2020-era Android phone and about a second for SwiftF0 including load, to be tested on a real device [inference].

Every option covers a 12-year-old's higher range [inference: ranges not verified]. Bigger beginner risks are breathy or wavering tone, which leaves gaps in the line, and twelve-semitone octave jumps; a clarity threshold, a median filter and snapping isolated 1200-cent leaps back reduce both [inference].

### Comparing two melody lines

Commercial scoring is unpublished. Vanido draws "a continuous orange line" against note bricks and scores notes afterward, and a reviewer found its scores hard to read as progress [secondary]. Singing Carrots says "slightly-off notes still count as hits" and calls a single 1–10 voice score "made up" [secondary, vendor blog]. Riyaz compares students with a reference lesson by an unpublished method [secondary], and no methods were found for Smule, Yousician or five other singing apps [inference: absence of evidence]. Academic systems publish theirs: Cantus gives full marks within a third of a semitone (about 33 cents), Qiu uses an adjustable tolerance, Tsai and Lee (2011) applied dynamic time warping over notes with 0.82 correlation to human judges, and TuneIn reports deviation in cents [secondary, via a 2026 survey]. The research yardstick for right pitch is within 50 cents [primary/verified, CREPE paper metric].

Converting each line to cents and subtracting its median removes the man-versus-child octave and any key choice in a few lines of code [inference]. Dynamic time warping, constrained to about a third of the phrase length, absorbs free rhythm; a cruder, more forgiving option compares only sequences of up/down/same moves, ignoring wobbles under about 50–80 cents [inference]. The proposed tolerances treat ±100 cents after centring as the same shape and over about 200 cents as different, showing nothing in between, and ignore timing except a clip twice as long or short as the reference [inference]. The display would be two overlaid lines, words beneath, no number [inference].

Seeing your own pitch helps, with caveats. Thirty-two seven-year-olds given a term of real-time pitch display improved pitch matching significantly more than controls (Welch, Howard and Rush 1989) [primary/verified, abstract]. Visual feedback groups improved markedly while controls didn't, though reading the display added mental load (Wilson 2006) [primary/verified thesis abstract; secondary for the 2008 follow-up]. Adult poor-pitch singers improved within one session only with visual feedback (Berglin, Pfordresher and Demorest 2022) [secondary, author lab page]. The display's detail must match the learner's skill (Hoppe et al. 2006) [secondary]. In one small study, musically untrained participants showed no significant gain [secondary, survey citing Piao and Xia 2022].

### Honesty risks

Trope is not karaoke. Each reader chants at their own pitch and tempo in free rhythm, and melismas stretch differently every time; centring and time warping handle much of this, but a correct chant can still look different [inference]. Leaving out the reference reader's ornament shows a difference that is not an error, and a learner taught another tradition's melody shows a mismatch that is not a mistake [inference]. Octave errors can make a correct note look 12 semitones off, and a child's breathier tone tracks less cleanly than an adult's [inference]. Noise and reverberant rooms raise error rates [primary/verified for reverberation, 2019 study]. Browsers apply echo cancellation by default, and Chrome also noise removal and auto-gain, which can smear sustained tones, so these should be requested off [secondary, 2017 test, dated]. Bluetooth headsets drop to a low-quality call codec when the mic is in use, so the phone's own mic is better [secondary]. The worst failure is a false "wrong": for a nervous 12-year-old, one red mark on a correct chant does more harm than no tool at all [inference]. Hence the framing: "Compare", never "Score"; two lines, no number, no red or green; a note that a higher or lower voice and a different pace are fine and what matters is whether the line rises and falls in the same places; nothing fed into progress tracking; a "couldn't hear a clear tune" message when the clip is mostly noise; and playback of both recordings so the learner's ear decides [inference].

### Privacy and safety

COPPA probably does not apply. The FTC FAQ (B.5) says it "applies to commercial websites and online services and not to nonprofit entities", except nonprofits operating "for the profit of their commercial members", and "encourages" nonprofits to follow it anyway [primary/verified]. A congregation is the clear non-commercial case, though a for-profit vendor running the app could change that [inference]. Even under COPPA, FAQ F.5 says an app is "not collecting personal information simply because your app interacts with personal information that is stored on the device and is never transmitted" [primary/verified]. An audio file of a child's voice, by contrast, has been personal information since 2013, and once gathered counts as collected even if later deleted [primary/verified, 2017 policy statement]. The 2025 amended rule (compliance date 22 April 2026) allows a narrow exception for a clip used only to answer the child's request and deleted at once, and adds "voiceprints" to personal information [primary/verified via Hunton and BBB summaries]. A pitch line held in browser memory and never sent is neither a voiceprint nor transmitted [inference]. State laws were not researched.

Keeping everything on the phone uses standard APIs: the mic feeds an in-memory buffer, analysis and playback run in the page, and on leaving, the mic tracks stop and the buffer is dropped. Nothing goes to IndexedDB or localStorage, no request or error log carries audio, and a strict Content-Security-Policy `connect-src` limits where the page could send data. Reference contours are precomputed from PocketTorah audio, so no learner data is involved there [inference, standard APIs]. The browser asks for the mic at least once per site [primary/verified, MDN]; iOS Safari tends to re-ask on later visits while Android Chrome remembers [inference: not verified]. An iframe's parent must allow `microphone` [primary/verified, MDN], iOS needs the AudioContext started from the Record tap [primary/verified, general rule], and code should read the real sample rate rather than assume 44.1 or 48 kHz [secondary/inference]. On-device-only processing very likely needs no legal consent [primary/verified sources; inference on application]; the browser prompt is itself a consent step, and a one-line notice by the button ("Your recording stays on this device and disappears when you leave this page — nothing is saved or sent") tells families plainly. Storing or uploading recordings would be a separate decision needing parental permission, a retention rule and a privacy-policy update [inference].

### Build size

The estimate is small for a first version and small-to-medium for a second [inference]. The first, "hear it, sing it, compare", has four parts: a recorder that turns off echo cancellation, noise suppression and auto-gain, captures into memory and caps at about 10 seconds; a pitch module running pitchy over 2048-sample frames at about a 10 ms hop, with clarity threshold, median filter, octave-jump fix and median-centred cents; reference contours precomputed at build time with the same code; and a screen that plays the reference, records, plays either or both, and draws two overlaid SVG lines over the words, with the "couldn't hear a clear tune" state and no storage. Its one dependency is pitchy (0BSD), and it should be tested on an older Android phone and an iPhone, in quiet and noisy rooms, with adult and child voices. A second version adds constrained time warping, aligns the learner's line to the reference, offers an optional per-word hint from the up/down/same comparison ("goes up here in the recording; yours stays level") with nothing shown in the 100–200-cent grey zone, and swaps in SwiftF0 if children's voices leave too many gaps. A percentage or star score, leaderboards, and storing or uploading recordings are not recommended at any stage [inference].
