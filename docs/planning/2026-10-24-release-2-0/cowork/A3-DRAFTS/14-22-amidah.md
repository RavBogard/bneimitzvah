## 14. T'filah opening, Adonai S'fatai (BM p. 13)

The Amidah, the "standing" prayer, is so central that the rabbis simply call it HaT'filah, the prayer. On Shabbat it has seven blessings: three that open, three that close, and one in the middle for the day itself. Taken together, they are a set of blessings around the gap between the world as it is and the world we are obliged to create. It opens with a single borrowed verse, *Adonai s'fatai tiftach*, "open my lips" (Psalm 51:17). Before we ask for anything, we ask only to be able to pray; even our words are something we receive before we offer them. Some of us take three small steps forward as we begin. To say this verse is to admit that the right words do not start with us.

(129 words.)

## 15. Avot v'Imahot (BM p. 14)

Avot v'Imahot, "ancestors," is the first blessing of the Amidah, and it begins with names. Its oldest form names only Abraham, Isaac and Jacob; egalitarian siddurim add Sarah, Rebecca, Leah and Rachel, and some go further, naming Bilhah and Zilpah, the handmaids of Genesis 30 whose sons became four of the twelve tribes. This blessing gives us our standing, the way a court gives a person standing: we do not come alone, but as heirs, connected to the past and obligated to the present because of it. It asks God to remember the faithful love of our ancestors, *chasdei avot v'imahot*. Some of us bow at its opening and closing words. To say these names is to take our place in a long line, and to accept that what they began is now ours to carry.

(139 words.)

## 16. G'vurot (BM p. 15)

G'vurot, "powers," is the second blessing. It praises the power that sustains life: holding up the falling, healing the sick, freeing the captive. Its traditional refrain is *m'chayeh hameitim*, "who revives the dead"; many Reform prayer books say instead *m'chayeh hakol*, "who gives life to all." Some siddurim print both, side by side, so each of us can say the words we can mean, and many hear either one as renewal. Either way, the blessing is about our obligations to the future: even the limits of a single lifetime do not exempt us from what we owe. It says God keeps faith "with those who sleep in the dust," and so do we, carrying on work begun by people who did not live to finish it. To say G'vurot is to accept a debt that runs past our own lifetime.

(142 words.)

## 17. K'dushah (BM p. 16)

The K'dushah, "holiness," is the third blessing as it sounds when the Amidah is said aloud together. It borrows the song the prophets imagined the angels singing, *kadosh, kadosh, kadosh* (Isaiah 6:3) and *baruch k'vod Adonai mim'komo* (Ezekiel 3:12), and makes it ours. It opens *Nekadesh et shimcha ba'olam*, "we will make Your name holy in the world": the words picture heaven, but the work is here. The K'dushah is about lifting ourselves and our world toward a more sacred calling. Tradition says it only with a minyan, because this holiness is made together, the room answering as one voice. Some of us rise on our toes at each *kadosh*. To sing it is to promise that holiness will not stay in the heavens; it is ours to bring into the world.

(132 words.)

## 18. V'shamru / K'dushat HaYom (BM p. 17)

On weekdays, the middle of the Amidah is thirteen blessings of need, for healing, justice, a good year, freedom, the gap in the world named one request at a time. On Shabbat they fall away, and one blessing for the holiness of the day, K'dushat HaYom, takes their place. At its heart is V'shamru (Exodus 31:16–17), the Torah's own words: Shabbat is a sign, the day on which even the Creator rested and *vayinafash*, "caught breath." The rabbis heard *nefesh*, soul, in that word. On Shabbat this blessing is about a world where everyone deserves rest, not as something earned but as something everyone is owed. To keep Shabbat is to live, for one day, as if that world were already here, and to remember everyone who is still denied it.

(131 words.)

Sources: "the middle blessings, which on weekdays are typically about the gap in the world; on Shabbat [it] is about a world where everyone is deserving of rest": Daniel, DECISIONS-LOG.md:12291 (C26), adapted; "(radical socialism!)" left out per tone rules. "not as something earned, but as something everyone is owed": CAPTIONS.md:255 (caption from his meaning note). V'shamru, *ot*, *vayinafash* / *nefesh*: shacharit/amidah.typ:90, :92 (house footnotes). ## 19. Avodah, R'tzei (BM p. 18)

R'tzei, "take pleasure," opens the three closing blessings. Its other name, Avodah, means "service," and it once meant the service of the Temple: the blessing asked for the sacrifices to be restored. Many Reform siddurim set that plea aside and keep the longing beneath it, that our prayer be received and that the *Shechinah*, the indwelling Presence, come home. We hear it as a prayer about what we do. Having stood in the gap between the world as it is and the world we are obliged to create, we ask that the things we do because we believe them to be for the good actually are for the good. It is our prayer that our impact matches our intentions. To say R'tzei is to hold our good intentions up to the light, and to be willing to learn we were wrong.

(142 words.)

## 20. Hoda'ah, Modim (BM p. 19)

Modim, "we give thanks," is the blessing of thanksgiving, and it is plainly about the ordinary: our lives, our souls, the miracles "that are with us every day," evening, morning and noon. It does not wait for something extraordinary. Some of us bow at its first word. When the Amidah is repeated aloud, tradition does not let the room simply listen; each person says a quieter Modim of their own, *Modim D'Rabbanan*, because gratitude, unlike a request, cannot be handed to someone else to say for you. Gratitude is also the beginning of obligation: to notice what we were given is to know we owe something back. To say Modim is to count what is already here before we go back to work on what is not.

(127 words.)

Sources: "Modim is thanksgiving": Daniel, DECISIONS-LOG.md:12291. ## 21. Sim Shalom (BM p. 20)

Sim Shalom, "grant peace," is the last blessing of the Amidah, the place it has been reaching all along. *Shalom* comes from the root for wholeness: not only the absence of war but a world with nothing missing. When the Amidah is repeated aloud, Birkat Kohanim, the priestly blessing (Numbers 6:24–26), comes just before it and also ends in peace. The morning service uses this longer Sim Shalom; the evening uses the shorter Shalom Rav. The blessing asks for peace for "all of us as one," and names what peace is made of: kindness, justice, blessing, life. Of all the gaps this prayer has named, this is the widest. To pray for peace is to agree to be one of the people who make it.

(128 words.)

## 22. Silent meditation, Elohai N'tzor and Oseh Shalom (BM p. 20)

After the fixed blessings, the Amidah opens into silence. Elohai N'tzor, "my God, guard," is a private meditation; the Talmud records it as what Mar bar Ravina said when he finished praying (Berakhot 17a). It turns from the "we" of the fixed blessings to a single "I," asking only to speak less harm and hold less pride. It ends with Psalm 19:15, "may the words of my mouth," and then Oseh Shalom, the same plea for peace that closes the Kaddish. Many versions widen that peace: for us, for all Israel, and *v'al kol yosh'vei tevel*, for all who dwell on earth. This is also the place for our own words, or for none. Some of us take three steps back and bow as we finish. We leave the Amidah as we entered it, one person at a time, and take its peace back out into the world.

(150 words.)
