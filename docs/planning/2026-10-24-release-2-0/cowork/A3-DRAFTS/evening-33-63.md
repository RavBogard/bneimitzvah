## BM48. Ma'ariv Aravim (p. 37) · NEW

Ma'ariv Aravim, "who brings on evening," is the first of the two blessings before the evening Sh'ma, the night's counterpart to the morning's Yotzeir Or. It praises not one act of creation long ago but the sky's daily turning: day rolling into night, the seasons changing, the stars set in their courses. Like its morning twin, it is a moment to take a cosmic perspective and feel our radical smallness. We are star dust, floating on a small pale blue speck; we are not the center of the story but a small puzzle piece. In its traditional words it closes by calling God living and enduring, unchanging through all time, and Jews have long called that unchanging order God. Darkness here is not the day's failure; it belongs to the same order as the light. To bless the evening is to trust the night as well as the day.

(147 words.)

Sources: "a cosmic perspective… radical smallness… star dust, floating on a small pale blue speck… not the center of the story; we are a small puzzle piece": Daniel, DECISIONS-LOG.md:12275 (R-0803-420), near-verbatim. "not one act of creation long ago but the turning of the sky": shabbat/maariv-before.typ:≈25 (house footnote). Two blessings before: Mishnah Berakhot 1:4. *El chai v'kayam*: the traditional closing line. "Jews have long called that… God": the approved Sh'ma's move. The approved Yotzeir Or uses the same images ("stardust on a small pale blue speck… a piece in a much larger puzzle"); this draft keeps Daniel's own wording and changes the frame, so the pair reads as twins, not a copy. ## BM49. Ahavat Olam (p. 38) · NEW

Ahavat Olam, "everlasting love," is the second blessing before the evening Sh'ma, the evening's form of the morning's Ahavah Rabbah. It names Torah and mitzvot, laws and judgments, as the shape love takes: to be taught is to be loved. Placed just before the Sh'ma, it makes the command to love an answer; we love because we were loved first. Love here means seeing ourselves as mutually obligated, to the past, to the present and most of all to the future. The blessing even borrows the Sh'ma's own words, promising to speak of these things when we lie down and when we rise up, so that study is not only for daylight. To say Ahavat Olam as night falls is to go into the dark already bound to one another.

(130 words.)

Sources: "obligated to the past, to the present, and most of all, to the future. that this is what it means to love — to see ourselves as mutually obligated": Daniel, DECISIONS-LOG.md:12277 (R-0803-421, C25), near-verbatim. "revelation as intimacy… we love because we have first been loved": shabbat/maariv-before.typ:≈38 (house footnote). *b'shochveinu uv'kumeinu* echoing Deuteronomy 6:7: the blessing's own words. Daniel's tikkun clause is left out here because approved Ahavah Rabbah already uses it. Last sentence Claude's.

## BM53. Hashkiveinu (p. 42) · NEW

Hashkiveinu, "lay us down," belongs only to the evening. In Ma'ariv the Sh'ma is wrapped in two blessings before and two after (Mishnah Berakhot 1:4), and this is the second after, a prayer to lie down in peace and rise again to life. It is a nighttime prayer, a prayer for when the darkness is so thick that it's hard to remember that there is light. But there is a light, even if we can't always see it. Its central image is the *sukkat shalom*, a shelter of peace spread over us. A sukkah has no solid roof; its shelter is real because people build it together. Hashkiveinu is a prayer of hope and comfort and belief in the possibility of shelter and peace in a world where sometimes it's hard to believe.

(149 words.)

Sources: the third, fourth and last sentences: Daniel, DECISIONS-LOG.md:12286 (R-0803-424, C12), verbatim apart from "It is a nighttime prayer". *sukkat shalom*, Shabbat ending with the canopy and not a plea for protection: shabbat/maariv-after.typ:≈52 (house footnote). Two before, two after: Mishnah Berakhot 1:4. Sukkah sentence Claude's, turning shelter toward what we make together (rule 4); cut it if the close should be his words alone.

## BM77. The wine, Yayin (p. 60) · NEW

Havdalah, "separation," is the ceremony that ends Shabbat, and it begins, as Shabbat began, with a cup of wine. The rabbis heard in the Torah's command to "remember" Shabbat a call to mark it with words of holiness at both ends: Kiddush as it arrives, Havdalah as it leaves (Maimonides, Mishneh Torah, Shabbat 29:1). The blessing itself is the simplest there is, thanks for the fruit of the vine, the same words said over every cup. Wine in Jewish life is the drink of celebration, and so the week does not slip back in unnoticed; we lift a cup to the day we are letting go. Some of us fill the cup until it spills over, a hope for a week of overflowing blessing. The first thing we do with an ending is honor what it held.

(137 words.)

Sources: nothing in Daniel's words on Havdalah (evening-sources.md §2). "remember" at entrance and exit: Mishneh Torah, Shabbat 29:1. Wine blessing as the first of the four on Saturday night: standard (and the ratified machzor Havdalah's order, havdalah-rh.typ). Overflowing cup: a common custom (rooted in Eruvin 65a's "wine poured like water"); offered as practice. First and last sentences Claude's.

## BM78. The spices, B'samim (p. 60) · NEW

The second blessing of Havdalah is over spices, *b'samim*, passed hand to hand so everyone can smell them. The rabbis imagined that each Shabbat brings a *n'shamah y'teirah*, an extra soul, which leaves as the day ends (Beitzah 16a), and the tradition offers fragrance to comfort us as it goes. Smell is the most physical of the blessings, and the most personal. We can hear the extra soul as what Shabbat makes possible: a day of rest owed to everyone, time that no one can take from us. As it leaves, we breathe it in once more, so that some of it comes with us into the week. Some of us keep the spice box open a moment longer than we need to.

(124 words.)

Sources: "sweet spices to revive us as the 'extra soul' of Shabbat slips away": havdalah-rh.typ:≈89 (house footnote, ratified by Daniel RH-1B2-6). Beitzah 16a (Resh Lakish): standard. "a day of rest owed to everyone": Daniel's Shabbat doctrine, DECISIONS-LOG.md:12291 (C26), borrowed as in approved §18. Last sentence Claude's.

## BM79. The light of the fire, Eish (p. 61) · NEW

The third blessing is over fire, *borei m'orei ha'eish*, "who creates the lights of fire." Kindling a flame is one of the kinds of work set aside on Shabbat, so this flame is the week's first act of making. A midrash says that on the first Saturday night, as darkness fell, the first human being was given the knowledge to strike two stones together and make fire (Pesachim 54a). Because the blessing names "lights" in the plural, the Havdalah candle is braided from several wicks into one flame. And because a blessing over light needs its use (Berakhot 53b), some of us hold our hands up to it and look at the light on our fingernails. The fire that would have broken Shabbat now begins the week. What we make this week can light or burn; the choice is ours.

(140 words.)

Sources: "a braided flame — the week's first fire, kindled the moment making things is permitted again": havdalah-rh.typ:≈89 (house, ratified). Pesachim 54a (fire created on the first Saturday night; Adam strikes two stones); Berakhot 53b; the plural *m'orei*; fingernails: standard. Last two sentences Claude's.

## BM80. The blessing of separation, Havdalah (p. 61) · NEW

The last blessing is Havdalah itself, *hamavdil bein kodesh l'chol*, "who separates holy from ordinary." It names the borders one by one: holy and ordinary, light and darkness, Israel and the peoples, the seventh day and the six days of making. The line about Israel can sound like ranking; we hear it, as in the Torah blessing, as a calling rather than a claim over anyone else. Judaism has always cared about lines in time, because a line is what lets a day be holy at all. The day was holy. The night is ordinary. Both are true, and we say so out loud. Some of us put out the flame in the last of the wine. Shabbat has shown us a world where everyone rests; the six days are where we are obliged to build it.

(138 words.)

Sources: "naming the differences one by one… The day was holy. The night is ordinary. Both are true, and we say so out loud.": havdalah-rh.typ:≈97 (house footnote, ratified by Daniel), verbatim for the three short sentences. "a calling rather than a ranking": approved §24. "Israel from the peoples" kept literal with framing in the note: R-0803-430 (DECISIONS-LOG.md:12318). Last sentence: C26 and C4 (DECISIONS-LOG.md:12291), borrowed. Flame in the wine: common custom.

## BM81. Eliyahu HaNavi (p. 62) · NEW

Eliyahu HaNavi, "Elijah the prophet," is sung as soon as Havdalah ends. In the Bible's telling Elijah never died; he went up in a whirlwind (2 Kings 2:11), and the prophet Malachi promised he would return before a great day, to turn the hearts of parents to their children and the hearts of children to their parents (Malachi 3:23–24). The Talmud assumes he will not come on Shabbat itself (Eruvin 43b), so Saturday night is the first moment he might, and we sing for him then. He is the herald of a redeemed world. We keep singing for that world even when we have trouble believing it is coming. At a Beit Mitzvah, with parents and children standing together, Malachi's promise is already happening in the room.

(131 words.)

Sources: II Kings 2:11, Malachi 3:24, "the herald of a world repaired": havdalah-rh.typ:≈107 (house, ratified). Eruvin 43b, and singing for him at the close of Shabbat: standard. ## BM82. Miryam HaN'viah (p. 62) · NEW

Miryam HaN'viah, "Miriam the prophet," is a modern song that sets Miriam beside Elijah at the end of Shabbat. The Torah calls her a prophet (Exodus 15:20); she led the women with a timbrel and song at the sea. The rabbis taught that a well followed the Israelites through the wilderness for her sake, and when she died, the water stopped (Ta'anit 9a). Elijah looks forward to a redeemed world; Miriam is a memory of what sustained the people on the way there. Adding her voice is part of a larger work, making sure everyone can see themselves in the tradition. Singing her name does not replace Elijah. It insists that the story of redemption has always had more than one prophet in it.

(124 words.)

Sources: nothing in Daniel's words. Exodus 15:20–21; Ta'anit 9a (Miriam's well): standard. "everyone can see themselves": C23 (charter :55-56). House precedent for adding Miriam beside Moses: shabbat/maariv-after.typ:≈36. The song's author is not named because the licensed page was not read. Interpretation Claude's.

## BM83. Shavua Tov (p. 62) · NEW

Shavua Tov, "a good week," is how Shabbat ends: not with a blessing over anything, but with a wish we say to each other. Its words are the most ordinary in the service, and that is the point. Havdalah has just named the border between holy and ordinary, and now we step across it together. The week ahead is not lesser time; it is where the work happens, the six days of making in which the world as it is might become a little more like the world we are obliged to create. Some of us sing it, hug, and say it person to person around the room. To wish someone a good week is to promise, quietly, to help make it one.

(123 words.)

Sources: nothing in Daniel's words; "fits the end of Shabbat": DECISIONS-LOG.md:1798-1799 (machzor ruling, uncertain). "the world as it is… the world we are obliged to create": C4 (DECISIONS-LOG.md:12291), borrowed. Interpretation Claude's.
