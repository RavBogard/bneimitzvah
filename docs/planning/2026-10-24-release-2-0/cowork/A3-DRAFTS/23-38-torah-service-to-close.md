## 23. Torah service opening, taking out the Torah (BM p. 21)

The Torah service begins when the ark is opened and the scroll is carried out. It is the most theatrical part of the morning, and it is meant to be: we celebrate the pageantry and majesty of text. The verses sung as the ark opens are gathered from the psalms. Vay'hi Binso'a is what Moses said each time the ark set out in the wilderness (Numbers 10:35), so words that once moved the ark through the desert now move it across the room. The Sh'ma is sung again, this time to a raised scroll, and the leader calls, *gadlu*, "magnify with me" (Psalm 34:4). Then comes the hakafah, the procession through the people. Some of us touch the scroll with the corner of a tallit or a prayer book as it passes, and bring it to our lips. The Torah does not wait at the front of the room; it comes to where we are.

## 25. Reading of the Torah (BM p. 22)

At the center of the Torah service is the reading itself. Each week has its portion, its parashah, and the traditional cycle moves through all five books in a year; many Reform congregations read a selection from each week's portion. The scroll is written by hand, with no vowels and no cantillation marks, so the reader learns the chant in advance from a printed text and then reads from the scroll, following the words with a *yad*, a pointer, so that no hand touches the letters. The reading is broken into aliyot, as person after person is called up to bless it. When Ezra read the Torah to the whole people, men, women and all who could understand stood to hear it (Nehemiah 8:2–3). Reading aloud, in public, makes the Torah belong to everyone in the room. To read it is to add one more voice to a chain more than two thousand years long.

(155 words.)

## 27. Mi Shebeirach, healing (BM p. 23)

Mi Shebeirach, "the one who blessed," takes its name from its first words, which call on the blessing given to our ancestors. While the Torah is still on the reading table, we pause to name the people in our lives who are ill, and ask for *r'fuah sh'leimah*, a complete healing, of body and of spirit. Those are two different things, and the prayer knows it: not every body can be cured, but no one has to be left alone with what they are carrying. Saying a name aloud in a full room turns a private worry into a shared one, and a shared worry becomes an obligation: to call, to visit, to show up. Behind it stands the oldest healing prayer in the Torah, Moses' five Hebrew words for his sister Miriam, *El na r'fa na lah* (Numbers 12:13). Healing here is something we ask for and something we do.

## 28. V'zot HaTorah (not in BM)

After the reading, the scroll is lifted high and opened wide, its columns turned toward the room. This is *hagbah*, the lifting, and as the scroll goes up the congregation sings V'zot HaTorah, "this is the Torah that Moses set before the people of Israel" (Deuteronomy 4:44), joined to a phrase from Numbers 9:23. Some of us point toward the open columns, with a little finger or the fringe of a tallit. The gesture is a way of saying that we saw it: not a story about the Torah but the words themselves. In Ashkenazi custom the lifting comes after the reading; in Sephardi custom the scroll is lifted before it. Either way, the moment belongs less to the person holding the scroll than to everyone looking at it together.

(130 words.)

## 30. Reading of the haftarah (BM p. 24)

The haftarah is the reading from the prophets, chanted after the Torah. It is usually read from a printed book rather than a scroll, and it has its own melody: the same cantillation marks as the Torah, sung to a different tune. Most weeks it is chosen to echo something in the Torah portion, a word, a theme, a story; some weeks the calendar chooses it instead. The prophets are the tradition's most uncomfortable voices. They named the gap between the world as it is and the world we are obliged to create, and they named it to kings, to priests and to crowds who did not want to hear it. When a student chants the haftarah, those words come back into a room of people who still have to decide what to do about them.

## 32. Commentary · Presentation (BM p. 25)

After the readings, the student who has just chanted them speaks about them. This is the d'var Torah, "a word of Torah": a short teaching on what the portion says and what the student makes of it. The reading has never been left to stand alone. When Ezra read the Torah to the people, the Levites helped them understand it, giving the sense of what was read (Nehemiah 8:7–8). Every commentary since, from the rabbis of the Talmud to the notes at the bottom of a modern Chumash, continues that work. The student's words join that conversation as one more voice in it, free to question what came before. This part of the service also makes room for a presentation from the congregation, the community's own word to the student.

(130 words.)

## 33. Returning the Torah, Eitz Chayim (BM p. 25)

When the reading is done, the scroll is carried back to the ark, often in a second procession through the room. The verses sung as it returns gather lines from Numbers, Psalms and Proverbs. Eitz Chayim comes from Proverbs (3:17–18), where wisdom is "a tree of life to those who hold fast to it," and all its paths are peace. A tree, not a monument: something living, that grows, gives shade and puts out new branches as each generation reads it. The last line, *hashiveinu*, "turn us back… renew our days as of old," is from Lamentations (5:21), the verse the tradition repeats after that book's bleak final line so it does not end in ruin. We borrow it to close the ark. The scroll goes home, and we ask to be turned back toward what it teaches.

## 34. Aleinu (BM pp. 26–27)

Aleinu, "it is upon us," began as part of the Rosh HaShanah service, a coronation prayer, and by the Middle Ages it had become the close of every service. Its language is royal: bending the knee, a sovereign over all, a day when every people will acknowledge the One. Those words were sung for centuries by Jews who had no power at all, and we hear them as a fantasy of power in times of powerlessness. Beneath the crown is something we can keep: a vision of a world where everyone could see themselves as obligated to each other, and to the same oneness. Its second paragraph gave the tradition the phrase *l'taken olam*, to repair the world. Some of us bow at *va'anachnu kor'im*. The prayer's first word already says whose work that repair is.

## 35. Mourner's Kaddish (BM pp. 28–29)

The Kaddish, "holy," is a prayer of praise in Aramaic, the everyday language many Jews once spoke, and it never mentions death. It praises God's great name, *sh'mei raba*, hopes for a world made whole, and ends in peace. It has become the prayer of mourners, said after a death and each year on the yahrzeit, the anniversary. Why a prayer that does not speak of loss? Perhaps because grief can make praise feel impossible, and the Kaddish asks a mourner to say it anyway, in the company of others. It needs a minyan, and its heart is not the mourner's words but the congregation's answer, *y'hei sh'mei raba m'varach*. The mourner speaks; the room answers. No one says the Kaddish alone, and no one grieves alone.

(127 words.)

## 36. Adon Olam (BM p. 30)

Adon Olam, "ruler of the world," is the closing of the service, and above all a musical moment: a short poem of trust, sung to countless melodies. It is often credited to Solomon ibn Gabirol, the poet of eleventh-century Spain, though no one knows for certain who wrote it. Its first lines are vast: the Eternal who reigned before anything was made and will remain when all is gone. Its last lines are close and small: *b'yado afkid ruchi*, "into God's hand I entrust my spirit," echoing Psalm 31:6, words many say at bedtime. The poem moves from the size of the universe to one person lying down to sleep, and finds the same oneness at both ends. Many siddurim also print it at the very start of the morning service, so the day can open and close with the same song.

(142 words.)

## 37. Parent's Blessing (BM p. 31)

Near the end of the service comes a moment that belongs to the family: the student's parents bless their child. An old midrash records very different words for this age. A father whose son reached thirteen was to say, "Blessed is the One who has released me from responsibility for this one" (Genesis Rabbah 63:10). A parent's blessing turns that around. It does not hand the child off. It says, out loud and in front of the community, what we hope for them, and it promises that our obligation to them keeps running forward, into a future we will not fully see. A child growing up does not end that bond; it changes its shape. To be blessed by the people who raised you is to hear, in public, that you were never carrying any of this alone.

(138 words.)

Sources: no teaching found (A3 file 37). Genesis Rabbah 63:10 (*baruch shep'tarani*): standard source. "obligation… most of all, to the future": C25 (DECISIONS-LOG.md:12277), predicted. ## 38. Closing blessing, Birkat Kohanim (BM p. 31)

The service closes with Birkat Kohanim, the priestly blessing (Numbers 6:24–26), the words Aaron and his sons were told to place upon the people. For most of Jewish history it belonged to the kohanim, who rose to bless everyone else, and in many communities they still do. It is a CRC tradition to end with it as a collective blessing that we give to each other: we turn to face one another, and the whole room blesses the whole room. In Hebrew its three lines grow, three words, then five, then seven, as if the blessing widens each time it is said, and it ends, like so many of our prayers, in *shalom*. No one here is only blessed, and no one only blesses.

(124 words.)
