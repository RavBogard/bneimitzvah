# Drafts 1: Bar'chu, Yotzeir Or, Ahavah Rabbah, Mi Chamochah

Voice model v0 + six approved examples. These describe the traditional prayers as the Shabbat Morning book prints them; CRC's Beit Mitzvah page (licensed, not read) may differ in a line.

## 08. Bar'chu (BM p. 7)

Bar'chu is the call to formal prayer, the moment the serious work of the service begins. The leader calls, the congregation answers, and a room of individuals becomes a community. The tradition requires a minyan for it (Mishnah Megillah 4:3): a person praying alone does not say Bar'chu, because it is a summons, and a summons needs someone to answer. Wherever Jews are in the world, this is the moment we reorient ourselves like spokes toward a center, so that facing the center also means facing one another. Some of us bow as we answer.

## 09. Yotzeir Or (BM p. 8)

Yotzeir Or, "who forms light," is the first of the two blessings before the Sh'ma. It begins with the oldest wonder there is, morning: light returning, and creation, as the blessing says, renewed every day. It is a moment to take a cosmic perspective and feel our radical smallness. We are stardust on a small pale blue speck, not the center of the story but a piece in a much larger puzzle. To bless the light is to remember how much was here before us, and how much does not depend on us at all.

Sources: DECISIONS-LOG.md:12275 (Daniel, verbatim images); CAPTIONS.md:240. Last sentence Claude's.

## 10. Ahavah Rabbah (BM p. 9)

Ahavah Rabbah, "great love," is the second blessing before the Sh'ma. Just before the Sh'ma asks us to love, this blessing insists that love has already been given: Torah itself is described as an act of love (the rabbis draw the link from how close these two prayers sit, Berakhot 11b). It is about the connections and obligations between us, to the past, to the present and most of all to the future. It speaks of being chosen, and we hear that as we do in the Torah blessing, as a calling to a task. Each of our souls, we can say, was made for a particular act of *tikkun*, repair. Some of us gather the four corners of the tallit here, to hold through the Sh'ma.

Sources: DECISIONS-LOG.md:12277 (Daniel: obligations; "each of our souls was created with a purpose, a specific act of tikkun"); shma-blessings-shacharit.typ:427 (house, Berakhot 11b, vocation); :419 (ritual mark).

## 13. Mi Chamochah (BM p. 12)

Mi Chamochah, "who is like you," is the song at the shore, the moment a people first sang itself free (Exodus 15:11). It sits inside the blessing of redemption, *ge'ulah*, which follows the Sh'ma and turns the Exodus into a present tense. The Hebrew around it is plain about the cost of that freedom, the firstborn of Egypt and the drowning at the sea, and it is honest to notice that. But the prayer leans toward a redemption still unfinished. That is why we rise from it straight into the Amidah, without a pause (Berakhot 9b): from a vision of a redeemed world into the work of making one.
