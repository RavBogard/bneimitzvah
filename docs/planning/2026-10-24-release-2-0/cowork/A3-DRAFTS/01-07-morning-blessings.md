## 01. Modeh Ani (BM p. 2)

Modeh Ani, "I give thanks," is the first prayer of the day, said the moment we wake, before we have even sat up. It is one of the youngest prayers in the morning, from the sixteenth century, and one of the shortest: thanks that the soul, lent back each night, has been returned. Because it comes before washing, it carries no divine Name and no blessing formula, yet it still speaks to God, borrowing *rabbah emunatecha*, "great is Your faithfulness," from Lamentations 3:23, where mercies are renewed every morning. Its first word comes in three forms: *modeh* (masculine), *modah* (feminine) and *modet* (non-binary), the third following the Nonbinary Hebrew Project's grammar, so that every one of us can say it truly. Before the day has asked anything of us, our first words are gratitude for another chance at life.

## 02. Tallit blessing (BM p. 2)

The tallit is a four-cornered prayer shawl, and its blessing is not really about the cloth but about the fringes on its corners, the tzitzit. The Torah asks for them so that we will look at them and remember (Numbers 15:39), and because that verse is about seeing, the tradition wears the tallit by day, which is why it belongs to the morning. The blessing thanks God, who makes us holy through mitzvot and commands us *l'hitateif batzitzit*, "to wrap ourselves in fringes." We can hear that command as a reminder that we are bound into obligations larger than ourselves, tied by the same strings to the past and to one another. Many egalitarian communities welcome anyone who wishes to wear one. Some of us wrap in it fully for a moment, saying its words softly. The tallit puts our reminders where we cannot help but see them.

## 03. Mah Tovu (BM p. 3)

Mah Tovu, "how good," is a prayer of arrival, said as we cross the threshold into prayer. Its first line belongs to Balaam (Numbers 24:5), a prophet hired to curse the Israelites, who looked down at their tents and found he could only bless them; the rabbis heard those tents as houses of prayer and study (Sanhedrin 105b). The verses that follow are stitched from the Psalms (5:8, 26:8, 95:6, 69:14), a small liturgy of coming in, bowing, and asking that this be a time of favor. It is a striking choice: our words of entering were first spoken by an outsider who came to see the worst in a people and saw the good instead. Some of us bow slightly as we say it. To begin with Mah Tovu is to look at the people around us and choose, as Balaam did, to see the blessing in them.

## 04. Hineih Mah Tov (BM p. 3)

Hineih Mah Tov is the first verse of Psalm 133, one of the Songs of Ascents (Psalms 120–134) that pilgrims are said to have sung on the road up to Jerusalem. Its subject is almost unbearably plain: how good and how pleasant it is when kin sit together. The Hebrew says *achim*, brothers; we hear it as all of us, family by birth and by choice. The psalm ends by saying that there, where people are together, God has commanded the blessing, as if the gathering itself were the place blessing lives. Sung near the start of the service, it does what it describes, turning a room of separate people into a community. Sitting together is not the warm-up to prayer. It is already one.

## 05. Birchot Hashachar (BM p. 4)

Birchot Hashachar, the "morning blessings," are blessings for the mundanely miraculous moments of the morning. The Talmud (Berakhot 60b) ties each one to an ordinary motion of waking: as the eyes open, we bless the One who opens the eyes of the blind; as we straighten up from bed, the One who lifts the bent; then dressing, standing, taking our first steps. Before a single great word is spoken, we notice the plain gift of a working body. Three of the blessings were once phrased as negations, thanks for what we were not made. Many Reform siddurim say them instead as gifts: made in the image of God, made free, made part of the people Israel, and some add a non-binary form, so that each of us can say the one that is ours. We begin, as we do every morning, with gratitude.

## 06. P'sukei D'zimrah (BM p. 5)

P'sukei D'zimrah, "verses of song," are the psalms and praises that carry us from waking toward Bar'chu. As Baruch She'amar opens this section, Yishtabach closes it; in between come psalms like Ashrei and Psalm 150, the final psalm, a call to express our joy with every instrument and every voice. This is a time for joy to come out in our bodies, in a visceral way, and not just our minds. Before the service asks us to think hard about what we say, it asks us to sing, and lets the music warm the room and open us up. Praying a shorter set of these psalms is itself traditional; the Shulchan Arukh already ranks which ones to keep when time is short (Orach Chayim 52:1). Some of us sway; some rise to dance. Here, praise is something the whole body is allowed to do.

## 07. Chatzi Kaddish (Reader's Kaddish) (BM p. 6)

The Chatzi Kaddish, the "half Kaddish," also called the Reader's Kaddish, marks a seam in the service: one movement closes, and the next begins. Here it seals the verses of song and turns us toward Bar'chu. Though the Kaddish is best known as a mourner's prayer, it names no death. It is written mostly in Aramaic, the everyday language of the Jews who shaped it, and its opening words, *yitgadal v'yitkadash*, echo Ezekiel 38:23. At its center is the room's answer, *y'hei sh'mei rabba m'varach*, "may God's great name be blessed," which is why it is said with a minyan. At heart it is a communal yearning for a world made whole. This shorter version stops before the closing lines for peace that the fuller Kaddish adds, and so it leaves that yearning open as we go on to pray.
