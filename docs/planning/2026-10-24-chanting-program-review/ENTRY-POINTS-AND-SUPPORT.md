# Different starting points, one chanting program

24 October 2026. Planning amendment following Daniel's clarification. **Binding entry assumption: basic Hebrew phonetic ability.** Designing flexible support for different prior abilities and knowledge is required; the mechanisms below are proposals for coordination with Claude Code, not implementation authorization.

## What we assume and what we teach

Assume learners can sound out pointed Hebrew. The core chanting course does not promise to teach the alphabet, vowels or initial decoding. It should not require a literacy course or begin by testing those foundations. People below that baseline may need separately identified support; they are outside this course's core outcome claim.

Basic decoding does not imply fluent reading of every Torah word, correct stress, knowledge of exceptional spoken forms, familiarity with trope, confidence singing, or ability to read unpointed text. Keep word-specific help available without treating the learner as someone who cannot read Hebrew. Reading from an unpointed scroll remains a later task where relevant, not an entry requirement.

The outcome becomes: help a person who can decode pointed Hebrew prepare and chant appropriate Torah, with a reusable preparation process and the support conditions their goal requires. The earlier concern that the course lacks a complete Hebrew-literacy bridge is no longer a core failure against this clarified promise. Production, feedback, joins, preparation, recovery and transfer remain relevant gaps.

## Useful entry profiles

These profiles describe starting situations. They are not fixed ranks, learner identities, ages or account roles. A person may fit more than one and change starting points for a different passage or tradition.

| Starting situation | Sensible entry | Useful support | Work to bypass when already secure |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Can decode Hebrew; new to chanting | First approachable real phrase, model and echo, then easy recognition of taught sounds | Stable voice, short repeats, word alignment, explicit practice/checking cues | Initial Hebrew literacy; no assumption of prior musical skill |
| Can learn a chant by ear or knows a memorized reading; little trope vocabulary | Prepare a small passage using a reference; connect recurring sounds to useful signs as needed | Phrase joins, independent recall, optional sound-to-sign explanations, a method for preparing the next reading | A mandatory naming catalogue or restarting at the first listening lesson |
| Knows some trope/signs; rusty or uneven in production | Relevant verse or passage, then targeted refresh of uncertain patterns | Easy accessible exemplars, difficult transitions, stress, endings and later retrieval | Relearning every familiar family or passing a broad vocabulary exam |
| Can already prepare ordinary readings; extending or refreshing ability | Own reading, difficult pattern, transition, scroll rehearsal or new tradition | Focused examples, special cases and self-directed practice | The introductory sequence and repeated proof of routine competence |

Experience in another trope tradition may help while also requiring new models. Do not mark a legitimate different tradition as inability or silently assume Torah and haftarah use the same melodies.

## Track needs separately rather than assign one level

The review should examine each of these dimensions:

- **Text use:** basic decoding is assumed; speed, word-specific stress and spoken forms can still need help.
- **Auditory familiarity and production:** recognizing a sound, echoing it and producing it without a leader are different abilities.
- **Signs and structure:** recognizing marks, knowing names and applying phrase patterns can differ substantially.
- **Integration:** isolated words or families, phrase joins, verse transitions and sustained reading need separate attention.
- **Preparation and correction:** someone may perform well with a teacher but lack a repeatable independent practice method.
- **Performance conditions:** pointed text, fewer cues, unpointed text and recovery are different demands.
- **Retention and transfer:** a well-memorized passage does not show how the person prepares another one; a rusty reader may recover quickly.

Confidence and musical background inform offered support, but should not determine placement. Do not equate little formal music knowledge with inability to chant, or fluent Hebrew with trope competence. Ear-first and sign-based approaches can be combined.

## How entry can work without an entrance exam

Offer a brief, optional starting choice in ordinary language: new to chanting; learned some by ear; know some trope and want a refresher; ready to work on a reading. Also allow direct entry to a reading and a visible way to change the suggested start or open any support.

Use an authentic short phrase to make the next recommendation useful. A model, an attempt and the learner's own report of where help is needed can guide a recommendation; the app must not imply that it heard or verified the voice. An experienced person can bypass this orientation. No forced account, microphone, recording, graded diagnostic, hard unlock or exhaustive questionnaire follows.

Do not resurrect the opening same/different test as placement. Recognition work comes after teaching its sounds. Keep the model voice stable initially; later variation should have a stated teaching purpose. A difficult cross-voice comparison must not be used to label someone a beginner.

## One common experience with flexible depth

Everyone has the same reading tools, teaching library and available practice. Suggested entry, explanation depth, repetition and cue level can vary. Learners can skip, revisit and ask for help without losing access or being demoted. Do not require a deterministic adaptive engine to implement this principle.

Start at a task the person can use, keep the complete preparation/performance loop, and spend practice where the difficulty occurs. An advanced learner may complete the loop with very little instruction; a newcomer may need many small supported attempts. Neither should be forced through the other's sequence.

This concerns instructional support, separate from ownership of a dated reading. An open shared reading does not assign its owner's skill profile to everyone who opens it. Personal learning/progress information remains private. Account and persistence choices require coordination; this proposal adds no new data model.

## What to review for each starting point

For each profile, inspect entry → useful first action → appropriate support → independent attempt → a next reading or next relevant challenge. Ask:

1. Is already-known material bypassable without a test the learner does not need?
2. Are actual missing skills taught, or merely assumed because of another strength?
3. Does a learner get a useful next action when they struggle or discover a gap?
4. Is the recommendation reversible, with direct access to reading and support?
5. Are completion and self-report distinguished from demonstrated ability?

Future authorized evaluation should include differing profiles within the basic-decoding population. Compare growth against each person's starting capability. For an experienced reader, efficient targeted preparation may be the relevant benefit; for a new chanter, producing and joining the first reading may be the immediate gain. A strong aggregate result must not hide a path that fails one starting group. No pilot or data collection is initiated here.
